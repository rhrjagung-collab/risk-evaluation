The Situation - You Are the Project Manager
Your role: You are the Consultant Project Manager visiting a tank farm construction site. It rained heavily last night. You are about to inspect the excavation area before work resumes.

Site Conditions This Morning

06:00 - Rain stops
Heavy rainfall throughout the night. De-watering pumps were running.
07:30 - Pre-work inspection
You arrive at the excavation area for the tank farm. The pit is deeper than 4 meters.
07:45 - First observation
You notice earth debris on the wiremesh rebar inside the pit. Something is not right.
07:50 - Second observation
You see cracks on the ground surface running along the line of the shoring piles.

What the Drawing Says vs. What You See

Shoring Pile Depth

Drawing Specification: 9M depth Contractor Report: 6M depth

The Contractor informs you that multiple piles were driven to only 6 meters instead of the required 9 meters. They did not inform anyone earlier. The reason: they could not achieve the required depth with their equipment.

Additional Information
  • Work is planned to resume today: de-watering and concrete bedding inside the pit
  • At least 5 workers will be inside the excavation during these activities
  • The Contractor site manager says: "The concrete will hold. We have done this before."
  • No formal review of the pile deviation has been done
  • The project is behind schedule by 15 days
What would you do? Before reading further, think about this: you are responsible for the safety of the workers and the integrity of the structure. What concerns do you have? What would you check next?

Identify Hazards From What You Observe

Based on the site conditions, identify what could cause harm. Each observation points to a potential hazard.

Hazard Identification for Excavation Pit > 4m

⚠

Ground Collapse

Shoring piles at 6M instead of 9M. Cracks on ground surface. Structural integrity is compromised. Soil movement is possible.

⚡

Energy-Based Hazards

Gravitational potential energy of unstable soil mass. If shoring fails, massive energy release onto workers below.

💧

Water Ingress

Heavy rainfall. De-watering ongoing. Water can further weaken soil and increase pressure on under-designed shoring.

🚧

Falling Objects

Earth debris on wiremesh rebar above working area. Loose material could fall onto workers during re-entry.

👥

Personnel in Hazard Zone

5 workers planned inside deep excavation. Limited escape routes if collapse begins. Egress time is critical.

🛠

Non-Conforming Structure

Design deviation (6M vs 9M) means the shoring is not fit for purpose. Actual capacity is unknown and unverified.

Understand the Risk Matrix

A risk matrix maps likelihood against consequence to produce a risk rating. Use it as a discussion tool, not a mechanical calculator.

Understanding Risk Matrix Limitations: Risk matrices are useful screening tools, but they can compress different risk levels into the same category. The multiplication of ordinal numbers produces pseudo-precise scores. Use the matrix to facilitate structured conversation, not to replace engineering judgment.

What Actually Happened

This Is a Real Incident

On 27 April 2019, this exact scenario played out at a real tank farm construction site. Here is what happened:

Night of 26 April
Heavy rainfall throughout the night. De-watering pumps running.
27 April - Morning inspection
Earth debris found on wiremesh rebar inside the pit. Cracks developed on ground along the shoring piles.
What was NOT done
No formal risk assessment of the pile deviation. No independent verification. No escalation to Client.
Root finding
Shoring piles driven to 6M instead of 9M. Multiple piles affected. Contractor knew but did not inform Consultant.

Root Causes

Why It Happened
  • Contractor did not inform Consultant that multiple piles could not reach 9M depth
  • Consultant Project Manager did not verify the piling process or quality
  • Contractor had a lack of appreciation of risk and altered the piling design without communication
  • Unrealistic project timeline without contingency caused Contractor to focus on progress and avoid rework
  • No formal Construction Readiness Review before Kick-Off Meeting

Lessons Learned

Key Takeaways
  • Communication: Any changes to design specification must be precisely communicated between Contractor and Consultant
  • Verification: Project Manager must verify critical safety information — do not rely on contractor assurances alone
  • Escalation: If you cannot resolve an issue, escalate to Client. Fear of consequences is not a reason to stay silent.
  • Construction Readiness Review: Must be completed and approved before project work commences
  • Risk Normalization: "We have done this before" is a red flag, not reassurance

The Care Factor

Seven Pillars of Care in Risk Management:
  1. Anticipation - Anticipate what could go wrong before it happens
  2. Recognition - Recognize warning signs (cracks, deviations from design)
  3. Responsibility - Take responsibility for safety of others in your care
  4. Escalation - Escalate concerns even when it is uncomfortable
  5. Communication - Communicate risks clearly and precisely
  6. Learning - Learn from near-misses and incidents
  7. Accountability - Hold yourself and others accountable

Reflection

If you chose Accept: The real outcome was that no one was inside the pit when cracks appeared. But work was about to resume. What would have happened if 5 workers were inside?

If you chose Mitigate or Avoid: You made the right call. The evidence (cracks, under-driven piles, no verification) clearly indicated an unacceptable risk. What specific actions would you have taken?

Key question: If you had shown care, how would you act differently? What information did you need that you did not receive?
